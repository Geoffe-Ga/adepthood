## "ABSORB (Do/Feel)"—Full 6-Phase Wavelength Breakdown

Ultraviolet's Wavelength is the rhythm of deepening realization. Each phase is a movement in the dance of awakening—and each has its medicine and poison.

### 1. RISING

**Rx: Unification of Mind | OD: Worldly Desire**

Rising at Ultraviolet is the mind gathering. You sit, you come back to the breath, and the scattered pieces of attention start drifting toward one point like iron filings toward a magnet.

**Unification of Mind** (*ekaggatā*, the jhana factor) is that gathering. It's the fruit of consistency: you sit every day, maybe you go on retreat, and slowly the mind stops being a committee. Thoughts still arise, but they arise *inside* a field that is mostly pointed one way. The breath gets vivid.

This is not forced. It's what attention does when you stop feeding its distractions.

But when the gathering mind gets pulled off by what it wants, Rising tips into **Worldly Desire**.

**Worldly Desire** is the first of the classical hindrances, and it's sneakier than it sounds. It's the mind reaching—for the bliss you had yesterday, for the retreat you're going to book. Every reach fragments the field again.

You see this in the practitioner whose sits are really shopping trips: scanning for the good state, bored by anything else.

The medicine is to notice the reaching as one more object and come back. Yes, gather. But gather *toward the breath*, not toward what you're hoping the breath will give you.

### 2. PEAKING

**Rx: Jhana | OD: Bliss Addiction**

Peaking at Ultraviolet is absorption. The gathered mind locks in. Pīti floods the body, sukha settles underneath it, and the breath, the body, and awareness stop having clear edges.

**Jhana** is that absorption. It's what the whole practice chapter has been building toward: a state stable enough to rest in, bright enough to see by.

Jhana is liberating. It proves the mind is trainable, and it gives insight somewhere to stand.

But when you start sitting *for* the state instead of *in* it, Peaking tips into **Bliss Addiction**.

**Bliss Addiction** is the ego's new favorite drug. "I touched the second jhana for twenty minutes." "Last retreat I was in it for hours." You start arranging your life around getting back there. Dry sits feel like failures. The practice has stopped being devotion and become a dealer.

You see this in the retreat junkie who can't do dishes, the person who talks about their states the way other people talk about their vacations.

The antidote is the instruction from the practice itself: don't grasp. The state comes when you stop chasing it, and leaves the moment you clutch. Treat jhana as weather you're grateful for, not a possession you're owed.

Jhana is medicine. Bliss Addiction is poison. Stay unhooked.

### 3. WITHDRAWAL

**Rx: Metta and Meditative Joy | OD: Agitation Due to Worry or Remorse**

Withdrawal at Ultraviolet is the state letting go of you. The absorption loosens. You're back in a body with knees, and what fills the space the bliss left is the real test.

**Metta and Meditative Joy** is the medicine. As the concentration releases, warmth stays: a tenderness toward the body that just sat, toward the mind that managed, for a while, to stop fighting itself, toward the people you'll walk out into, every one of whom is leaning on a note that once ran your own life. Joy here isn't the fireworks of pīti. It's quieter: gladness at having been somewhere good.

This is why the traditions pair samatha with lovingkindness. Concentration opens the heart's hand; metta is what you put in it.

But when the mind comes back and starts picking at itself, Withdrawal tips into **Agitation Due to Worry or Remorse**.

**Agitation Due to Worry or Remorse** is the hindrance that shows up after the high: the restless rerun. "Did I waste that sit?" "I should have stayed longer." "I said that stupid thing last week." The body that was still ten minutes ago is twitching, and the mind is auditing its whole life.

You see this in the practitioner who gets up from the cushion and reaches straight for the phone to check whether they've done something wrong. The absorption gave them stillness, and the first thing they did with it was worry.

The medicine is to meet the agitation with the same metta you're meant to be feeling. Yes, the state is gone. Wish yourself well anyway. Let the joy be for the sit that *was*, not the one you're grading.

### 4. DIMINISHING

**Rx: Sustained Attention | OD: Doubt**

Diminishing at Ultraviolet is the long middle. The states have faded, the glow has faded, and what's left is the plain work of sitting down again tomorrow.

**Sustained Attention** (*vicāra*) is the medicine. Not the sharp first contact with the breath, but the staying. Breath after breath, sit after sit, week after week. This is where the practice becomes yours instead of borrowed: you keep attention on the object not because it's interesting but because you said you would.

But when the staying starts asking whether any of this is real, Diminishing tips into **Doubt**.

**Doubt** is the hindrance the Buddha put last on the list because it's the one that dismantles the rest. "Maybe I imagined the jhana." "Maybe this tradition is wrong." "Maybe I'm not the kind of person this works for." It rarely announces itself as doubt. It announces itself as reasonableness.

The antidote is trust, in the specific sense of staying on the object through one more sit. You've tasted this. Let the memory of the taste carry you through the dry weeks. You don't need to be certain to be sitting.

### 5. BOTTOMING OUT

**Rx: Pleasure | OD: Aversion**

Bottoming Out at Ultraviolet is the floor. The states are a memory. The practice feels dry, mechanical, pointless. This is the Dark Night in miniature.

**Pleasure** (*sukha*) is the medicine, and it's the least obvious one on the map. At the bottom, the instruction isn't to grit your teeth. It's to find the one small pleasant thing—the warmth of your hands, the weight of the body on the cushion, the slight ease on the out-breath—and rest there. The jhana teachers say it plainly: the mind will not settle into something it dislikes.

This is why Ultraviolet's habit is about what you eat. The floor is where you learn that contentment is not a reward for practice. It's an ingredient.

But when the mind starts pushing against what's here, Bottoming Out tips into **Aversion**.

**Aversion** is the hindrance of the clenched jaw. You hate the sit. You hate the dryness. You hate your knees, the teacher, the whole tradition. And aversion is self-sealing: the harder you push against the floor, the harder the floor gets.

You see this in the practitioner who white-knuckles through retreat, sitting out of spite—and in the one who quits in disgust. Same movement, pointed different ways.

The medicine is softening. Yes, it's dry. Find the one pleasant thing anyway. Let the body like something. That's not bypassing the difficulty. It's giving the mind a reason to stay.

### 6. RESTORATION

**Rx: Directed Attention | OD: Laziness or Lethargy**

Restoration at Ultraviolet is where you start again. You've been through the cycle—the gathering, the bliss, the fade, the floor. Now the mind needs to be pointed somewhere, on purpose, one more time.

**Directed Attention** (*vitakka*) is the medicine. It's the deliberate act of placing the mind on the breath. Not sustaining yet—just aiming. You sit down and you choose the object. That choice, made fresh after a dry spell, is the whole phase.

But when the aim goes slack, Restoration tips into **Laziness or Lethargy**.

**Laziness or Lethargy** is the hindrance of the heavy lid. You sit, but you don't point. The mind drifts, dozes, sinks into a pleasant fog that feels like meditation and isn't. Or you skip the sit—"I'll do it tomorrow"—and tomorrow has the same weight. This isn't rest. Rest restores.

You see this in the practitioner coming off a retreat who coasts for weeks on the memory: sitting shorter, then skipping, then "meaning to."

The antidote is small and bodily. Sit up. Open the eyes a little. Take three sharp breaths. Pick the object again. You don't need motivation—you need to aim once, and then once more.

Directed Attention is medicine. Laziness or Lethargy is poison. Point the mind, gently, again.
