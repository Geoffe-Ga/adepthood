## Green's Core Task: Find a Group to Practice Belonging With

The Shadow Work Ritual is something you do alone. Green has a second core task, and you can't do it alone: go out and find a group of real people, and keep showing up until you belong to it.

I'll argue for this plainly. Shadow work done only in private drifts toward the Shadow Glorifier: a fluent vocabulary, an excellent map, and nobody who has ever watched you cry. A group doesn't fix that by doing anything. It fixes it by staying in the room while you're a person. Shame needs secrecy, and a regular gathering of people who keep coming back is one of the plainest ways I know to interrupt it. Nothing locks if you skip this. What you lose is the witness.

If you're like me, you've talked yourself out of a group more than once. This chapter is for that part of you too.

### What *Bowling Alone* Noticed

In 1995 the political scientist Robert Putnam published an essay called "Bowling Alone," and in 2000 he expanded it into a book, *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community*. One pair of numbers carries the argument: between 1980 and 1993 the total number of bowlers in America rose about 10 percent, while league bowling fell about 40 percent. People hadn't stopped bowling. They'd stopped bowling with the same people, on the same night, every week.

Putnam's point is that the league was never really about the bowling. It was a machine for producing what he calls *social capital*: the trust and reciprocity that build up among people who keep running into each other. Nobody joined a league to build trust. The trust was a byproduct of showing up. When the structure went, the byproduct went with it.

He draws a useful distinction, too. *Bonding* ties connect people who are alike, like a recovery meeting or an affinity group. *Bridging* ties connect people who differ, like a hobby club or a league. You'll want both eventually. Either will do to start.

And one more, offered carefully. In the health research he surveys, Putnam gives a rule of thumb: someone who belongs to no groups and joins one roughly halves their odds of dying in the following year. That's an association, not a promise, and people who join groups differ from people who don't. But it suggests the effect isn't small.

### Choose a Group

The menu is wide. I found Recovery Dharma, a peer-led, Buddhist-inspired recovery community whose meetings typically combine meditation, reading, and sharing. You might find AA or another twelve-step fellowship, a Yogananda meditation group, a hobby group (climbing, mycology, knitting, tabletop games), an affinity group of people who share an identity or experience with you, a special-interest group (a book club, an astronomy society, a coding meetup), or a bowling league. The league is not a joke. It's the whole thesis.

Five questions help you choose:

1. **Does it meet on a regular schedule?** Recurring beats impressive.
2. **Is it small enough that someone would notice you were gone?**
3. **Is it organized around something other than you?** A shared activity or purpose gives your attention somewhere to go besides being watched.
4. **Can you honestly claim its stated purpose?** A recovery meeting is for people in recovery. If a meeting is open, it will say so. Don't walk into a room under false pretenses.
5. **Can you imagine attending ten times?** Not loving it. Attending.

Don't wait for the perfect one. Pick a good-enough group and treat it as an experiment. Online counts. Where you can, add one where bodies share a room, because Green is about understanding that lives in the body.

### Then Help Yourself Fit In

None of the following will make you interesting. That's the point.

**Show up. Then show up again.** This is the biggest aid, and it isn't close. Familiar becomes safe, for them and for your nervous system. For a long while, being the person who is there on Thursdays is the whole job.

**Volunteer to provide a service.** Set up chairs, make the tea, keep time, run the Zoom. Commitment builds belonging: a small repeated job turns you from a guest into part of how the thing works. It isn't the price of your seat; nothing is. It's how you stop being a visitor. Pick something boring and repeatable. If you're volunteering to be seen as helpful, that's the old currency in a new coat.

**Share only when it's truly appropriate.** Early on, listen. When you do share, keep it brief, true, and relevant, and share what you've already metabolized rather than what's still raw. Raw goes to one trusted person or a therapist, not the circle (that's Oversharing, from Rising). Before you speak, ask: Am I moved to say this, or anxious? Does it serve the room, or only my need to be seen?

M. Scott Peck's *The Different Drum* is a good teacher here. It describes groups passing from polite pseudocommunity through chaos into *emptiness*, a letting go of the preconceptions and ego-driven barriers that keep you out, and then into real community. Emptiness is the zero self-expectations of Belonging, seen from the group's side. Community Building, the workshop practice that grew out of the book, is a formal way to practice it: mindfulness applied to how you take part in a group, noticing the urge to speak, to impress, or to flee, and choosing.

**Come early and stay late.** The real conversations happen at the edges, before the meeting starts and after it ends, when the format dissolves and people become people. Early also gets you the small service. Late is how a room turns into names.

**Ask more questions than you do anything else.** People love talking about themselves, and a question is the lightest possible way into someone. It also takes you off the stage, because nobody can perform while they're genuinely curious. Ask the one you want the answer to. People can tell the difference.

**Give it time.** It took me nine months of regular Recovery Dharma meetings before I even talked to someone after a meeting. I'm not telling you that to discourage you. I'm telling you so that if it takes you nine months, you know nothing has gone wrong. Notice, too, that this stage lasts three weeks and belonging doesn't. What three weeks can do is start the repetition. The capacity stays after the stage ends.

### What It Can Feel Like

I cried more than once on this road, convinced I was too liminal to ever fit in. Then something happened that I still can't fully explain: my exact wounds seemed to be called forward, to be addressed. The synchronicities in a group, where the whole field seems to bring to bear what particular members need, are bizarre and powerful. I don't know the mechanism. Maybe it's a field. Maybe it's what happens when many honest people speak about the same few human things in one room and you finally listen. You don't have to pick an explanation.

This is where Green's Divine Feminine comes in: receptivity. Watch for the signs a group gives that you're welcome and that it's committed to your growth. A name remembered. A seat saved. Someone asking how your week went. Being noticed when you're absent. Being handed a small job. They're easy to miss and easy to argue with. Alienation will say they were just being polite, and Social Anxiety will say they haven't met the real you yet. The practice is to receive one sign per meeting and not argue with it.

One caution: feeling seen is not proof a group is safe. Purple's shadow, delusions of reference, produces that feeling, and so do some manipulative groups. Use the next section too.

### Beware of Cults, and of the Other Mistake

There are two mistakes here, and both feel like wisdom. One is joining anything that says yes. The other is writing off anything that asks anything of you.

The second is the Liminal Creep's specialty. The first time a group asks you to loosen your grip, to take a turn, follow the format, wait while someone else is wrong, or be one of twenty, part of you hears a cult alarm. Sometimes it's right. Usually it's a group being a group. Not every request to merge a little and relax the ego is corrupt. Some of those requests are the whole medicine.

The difference is *what* is being asked. A healthy group asks you to loosen your performance. A controlling one asks you to give up your judgment, your outside relationships, your money without limit, or your freedom to leave. Some questions to ask:

- Can I leave, and come back, without penalty or shame? How does the group talk about people who left?
- Is money transparent, modest, and optional?
- Are my outside relationships, work, doctors, and therapist treated as fine, or as threats?
- Can I disagree or ask hard questions without being told I'm damaged, asleep, or resisting?
- Is leadership accountable to anyone?
- Is there pressure to commit faster than I'm ready: intense warmth at the start, deadlines, "now or never"?
- Does it claim to be the only way, the only truth, or the only real community? (That's Megalomania at the scale of the circle.)

Steven Hassan's BITE model, which looks at behavior, information, thought, and emotional control, is a widely used framework for judging how much control a group exerts. Treat any checklist as a prompt for your own judgment, not a verdict.

Your body helps, too. The discomfort of being ordinary, meaning boredom, awkwardness, exposure, and a little grief, usually loosens as you keep coming back. The discomfort of being controlled, meaning dread about leaving, secrecy, shrinking, and urgency, usually tightens. If a group is wrong, leave, tell someone outside it, and don't read it as a failure. A wrong group isn't evidence that groups don't work. Go back to the menu.

### The Task

Pick one group this week. Go to three meetings over the next three weeks. Come early once. Ask a question at each. That's all the stage asks of you. After that, the group is yours to keep or not.

The room has room. Go find out which one.
